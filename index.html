Q1 Introduction to Petrochemicals

(a) Definition of Petrochemical

A petrochemical is any chemical compound or product derived from petroleum (crude oil) or natural gas through refining and chemical processing, rather than being extracted directly as fuel. They serve as the building blocks for plastics, synthetic fibres, rubbers, solvents, detergents and many other industrial chemicals.

(b) Three Categories of Primary Petrochemicals

  1. Olefins (Aliphatics): ethylene, propylene, butylenes and butadiene — obtained mainly by steam cracking.
  2. Aromatics: benzene, toluene and xylenes (BTX) — obtained mainly by catalytic reforming.
  3. Synthesis gas (Syngas): a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen (CO + H₂) obtained by gasification/steam reforming, used to make methanol and ammonia.

(c) Four Methods for Production of Primary Petrochemicals

  • Steam cracking — thermal decomposition of hydrocarbons (ethane, propane, naphtha) at high temperature to give olefins.
  • Catalytic cracking — breaking of heavier hydrocarbon fractions using a catalyst (e.g. zeolites) at lower temperature than thermal cracking.
  • Catalytic reforming — conversion of naphtha into aromatics (BTX) using platinum-based catalysts.
  • Gasification / Partial oxidation — reaction of hydrocarbons or coal with limited oxygen/steam to produce synthesis gas.

(d) Classification of Petrochemicals Based on Raw Material Source

  • Natural gas-based petrochemicals — derived from methane, ethane, propane (e.g. ammonia, methanol).
  • Petroleum (liquid)-based petrochemicals — derived from naphtha and gas oil (e.g. olefins, aromatics).
  • Coal-based petrochemicals — derived from coal gasification/carbonisation (less common today).

(e) Types of Petrochemicals Based on Chemical Structure

  • Aliphatic (olefinic) petrochemicals — straight/branched chain, e.g. ethylene, propylene.
  • Aromatic petrochemicals — ring-structured, e.g. benzene, toluene, xylene.
  • Diolefins — two double bonds, e.g. butadiene.
  • Synthesis-gas-based petrochemicals — e.g. methanol, ammonia.

Q2 Ethylene (Ethene)

(a) Five Reasons Ethylene is "The King" of Petrochemicals

  1. It is produced in the largest volume of all petrochemicals worldwide.
  2. It is highly reactive due to its C=C double bond, allowing many types of reactions (addition, oxidation, polymerisation, halogenation).
  3. It is the starting material for the widest range of derivatives (polyethylene, ethylene oxide, ethylene glycol, vinyl chloride, styrene, ethanol).
  4. It is relatively cheap and easy to obtain from steam cracking of abundant feedstocks (ethane, naphtha).
  5. It has a simple structure that makes downstream processing and purification straightforward.

(b) Production of Ethylene through Steam Cracking

Feedstock (ethane, propane or naphtha) is mixed with steam and passed through tubes in a furnace heated to about 750–900 °C. The high temperature causes thermal cracking (breaking of C–C and C–H bonds), converting the saturated hydrocarbons into ethylene and other olefins. The hot gas is rapidly quenched to stop further reaction, then compressed and sent to a fractionation train where ethylene is separated from propylene, methane, hydrogen and other by-products.

C₂H₆ (ethane) → C₂H₄ (ethylene) + H₂ (steam, ~800 °C)

(c) Five Important Uses of Ethylene

  • Production of polyethylene (LDPE/HDPE) for plastics and packaging.
  • Production of ethylene oxide (→ ethylene glycol, antifreeze, polyester fibres).
  • Production of vinyl chloride monomer (→ PVC).
  • Production of styrene (via ethylbenzene) for polystyrene and SBR.
  • Production of ethanol by hydration.

(d) Production of Ethylene Oxide

Ethylene is oxidised directly with oxygen (or air) over a silver catalyst at moderate temperature and pressure.

2 C₂H₄ + O₂ --(Ag catalyst)--> 2 C₂H₄O (ethylene oxide)

(e) Four Uses of Ethylene Oxide

  • Manufacture of ethylene glycol (antifreeze and polyester/PET fibre production).
  • Manufacture of surfactants and detergents.
  • Used as a sterilising agent for medical equipment.
  • Solvent and intermediate in the chemical industry.

Q3 Propylene

(a) Production of Propylene by Steam Cracking

Propylene is obtained as a co-product of ethylene production. Propane or naphtha feedstock is cracked with steam at high temperature (~750–850 °C) in a furnace, breaking C–C bonds to yield a mixture of olefins. The cracked gases are quenched and fractionated, and propylene is separated from ethylene, methane and heavier fractions by distillation (the propylene/propane splitter).

(b) Five Uses of Propylene

  • Production of polypropylene (packaging, textiles, automotive parts).
  • Production of propylene oxide (→ polyurethane, propylene glycol).
  • Production of acrylonitrile (→ acrylic fibres, ABS resin).
  • Production of cumene (→ phenol and acetone).
  • Production of isopropanol (rubbing alcohol, solvent).

(c) Propylene Oxide via the Chlorohydrin Process

Propylene reacts with chlorine and water to form propylene chlorohydrin, which is then treated with a base (e.g. calcium hydroxide) to eliminate HCl and form propylene oxide.

CH₂=CHCH₃ + Cl₂ + H₂O → CH₂(OH)CHClCH₃ (propylene chlorohydrin) CH₂(OH)CHClCH₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → propylene oxide + CaCl₂ + H₂O

(d) Propylene Oxide via the Hydroperoxide Process

Propylene reacts with an organic hydroperoxide (such as ethylbenzene hydroperoxide or tert-butyl hydroperoxide) in the presence of a catalyst. The oxygen atom is transferred to propylene to form propylene oxide, while the hydroperoxide is reduced to an alcohol (e.g. styrene or tert-butanol as co-product).

CH₂=CHCH₃ + R-OOH --(catalyst)--> propylene oxide + R-OH

(e) Four Uses of Propylene Oxide

  • Manufacture of polyether polyols for polyurethane foams.
  • Manufacture of propylene glycol (used in food, cosmetics, antifreeze).
  • Manufacture of surfactants.
  • Solvent and chemical intermediate.

Q4 Butylene Oxide, Polybutadiene, SBR & Neoprene

(a)(i) Production of Butylene Oxide

Similar to propylene oxide, butylene can be converted to butylene oxide via the chlorohydrin route (chlorination/hydration followed by dehydrohalogenation) or via catalytic epoxidation with a hydroperoxide.

CH₃CH=CHCH₃ + Cl₂ + H₂O → butylene chlorohydrin Butylene chlorohydrin + Ca(OH)₂ → butylene oxide + CaCl₂ + H₂O

(a)(ii) Two Uses of Butylene Oxide

  • Used as a chemical intermediate for making polyols/polyurethanes.
  • Used as a solvent and stabiliser in chlorinated compounds.

(b) Two Types of Polymerisation for Polybutadiene

  • Solution polymerisation: butadiene is polymerised in an organic solvent using Ziegler-Natta or organolithium catalysts, giving highly stereoregular cis-1,4-polybutadiene with good elasticity — mainly used in tyres.
  • Emulsion polymerisation: butadiene is polymerised as droplets dispersed in water using free-radical initiators (e.g. persulfate), giving polybutadiene with a more random/mixed structure — used for general-purpose rubber goods.

(c)(i) Production of SBR (Styrene Butadiene Rubber)

SBR is produced by the copolymerisation of styrene and butadiene monomers (roughly 1:3 ratio), usually by emulsion (free-radical) polymerisation, though solution polymerisation is also used.

n CH₂=CHC₆H₅ (styrene) + n CH₂=CH-CH=CH₂ (butadiene) --(polymerisation)--> SBR copolymer

(c)(ii) Three Applications of SBR

  • Manufacture of vehicle tyres and tyre treads.
  • Footwear soles.
  • Adhesives, conveyor belts and gaskets.

(d) Production of Chloroprene and Neoprene

Chloroprene (2-chloro-1,3-butadiene) is produced from butadiene by chlorination followed by dehydrochlorination (isomerisation). It is then polymerised by free-radical emulsion polymerisation to form neoprene (polychloroprene).

CH₂=CH-CH=CH₂ + Cl₂ → 3,4-dichlorobut-1-ene → (dehydrochlorination) → CH₂=CCl-CH=CH₂ (chloroprene) n Chloroprene --(polymerisation)--> Neoprene (polychloroprene)

(e) Five Differences Between SBR and Neoprene

Q5 Ethylbenzene, Styrene, LABS, TNT, TPA/DMTA

(a)(i) Production of Ethylbenzene

Ethylbenzene is produced by alkylation of benzene with ethylene, typically using a Friedel-Crafts catalyst (AlCl₃) or a zeolite catalyst at elevated temperature and pressure.

C₆H₆ (benzene) + C₂H₄ (ethylene) --(AlCl₃ / zeolite)--> C₆H₅C₂H₅ (ethylbenzene)

(a)(ii) Two Applications of Ethylbenzene

  • Main feedstock for styrene production.
  • Used as a solvent in industry.

(b)(i) Production of Styrene from Ethylbenzene

Styrene is produced by catalytic dehydrogenation of ethylbenzene, passing it with steam over an iron oxide catalyst at high temperature (~600 °C), removing hydrogen.

C₆H₅CH₂CH₃ --(Fe₂O₃ catalyst, steam, heat)--> C₆H₅CH=CH₂ (styrene) + H₂

(b)(ii) Two Uses of Styrene

  • Production of polystyrene (packaging, insulation, disposable products).
  • Production of SBR and ABS resins.

(c)(i) Production of Linear Alkylbenzene Sulfonate (LABS)

Linear paraffins are dehydrogenated to linear olefins, which are used to alkylate benzene (Friedel-Crafts alkylation) to form linear alkylbenzene (LAB). The LAB is then sulfonated using sulfur trioxide (SO₃) or oleum to produce linear alkylbenzene sulfonic acid, which is neutralised (e.g. with NaOH) to give LABS.

Benzene + linear olefin --(AlCl₃/HF)--> LAB LAB + SO₃ → LAB-sulfonic acid --(NaOH)--> LABS (sodium salt)

(c)(ii) Two Uses of LABS

  • Main active ingredient in household and industrial detergents.
  • Used as a surfactant/wetting agent in cleaning products.

(d)(i) Production of Trinitrotoluene (TNT)

TNT is produced industrially by stepwise nitration of toluene using a mixture of concentrated nitric acid and sulfuric acid (mixed acid), which progressively introduces nitro groups at the 2, 4 and 6 positions of the ring, forming mono-, di-, and finally tri-nitrotoluene.

C₆H₅CH₃ (toluene) + 3 HNO₃ --(conc. H₂SO₄)--> C₆H₂CH₃(NO₂)₃ (TNT) + 3 H₂O

(d)(ii) Two Uses of TNT

  • Used as a military and industrial explosive (blasting, mining, demolition).
  • Used as a reference standard in explosive-yield chemistry.

(e)(i) Production of TPA and DMTA

Terephthalic acid (TPA) is produced by the catalytic oxidation of para-xylene with air/oxygen, using a cobalt/manganese acetate catalyst with a bromide promoter (Amoco/Mid-Century process).

p-xylene + 3 O₂ --(Co/Mn/Br catalyst)--> Terephthalic acid (TPA) + 2 H₂O

Dimethyl terephthalate (DMTA) is produced by esterifying TPA with methanol (or by oxidising p-xylene and esterifying the intermediate directly).

TPA + 2 CH₃OH --(acid catalyst)--> Dimethyl terephthalate (DMTA) + 2 H₂O

(e)(ii) Two Applications of TPA/DMTA

  • Manufacture of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) for synthetic fibres (polyester) and plastic bottles.
  • Production of polyester films used in packaging and photographic/recording tape.
